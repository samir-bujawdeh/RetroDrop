How to get photos off an old iPhone onto a Mac in 2026

Old iPhones are popular again for their camera look. Getting the photos off them is the hard part: AirDrop never existed on them, Photo Stream has been shut down, and modern websites won't load in their old browsers. Here's every method that still works, and when to use each.

The short answer

  • Have a 30‑pin cable? Plug the phone into your Mac and use Image Capture. It's built into macOS and needs nothing else.
  • No cable, or you'd rather go wireless? Use a Wi‑Fi tool that works with old iOS, like RetroDrop (free). It works on iOS 6 through Safari, and on iOS 5 and earlier through the Mail app.
  • Hoping for AirDrop, iCloud or Photo Stream? None of them are available on these phones anymore.

1. USB cable with Image Capture or Photos Works

Old iPhones show up on a Mac as a digital camera, so macOS's built-in tools can import from them over USB, even on the latest macOS.

  1. Connect the iPhone with its USB cable (30‑pin or Lightning, depending on the iPhone) and unlock it.
  2. Open Image Capture (press ⌘‑Space and type "Image Capture").
  3. Select the iPhone in the sidebar, choose a destination folder, and click Download All, or select only the photos you want and click Download.

The Photos app can import the same way, but it puts everything into your Photos library. Image Capture is better if you want plain files in a folder.

Good for: importing everything at once, very large videos, and phones with no working Wi‑Fi.
Downsides: you need the right cable, and old 30‑pin cables are getting hard to find. You also have to plug in every time.

2. Over Wi‑Fi with RetroDrop Works

RetroDrop is a free, open‑source Mac menu bar app made for exactly this. It turns your Mac into a small server on your home Wi‑Fi that old iPhones can still talk to, so there's nothing to install on the phone:

  • iOS 6 and later: open a simple page in Safari, pick photos or videos, tap Upload.
  • iOS 5 and earlier: Safari can't upload files on these versions, so instead you "email" the photos from the Photos app. RetroDrop acts as the mail server and keeps the attachments. Nothing is actually emailed.

New photos appear in your Mac's menu bar, ready to drag onto the desktop or into any app.

Good for: sending a few photos at a time without a cable, which is handy if you shoot with an old iPhone regularly.
Downsides: it needs a Mac on macOS 14 or later, and both devices on the same Wi‑Fi. It isn't notarized by Apple yet, so macOS asks you to confirm the first time you open it.

3. Emailing photos to yourself Often fails

On paper, you can open a photo, tap Share → Email Photo, and send it to your own address. In practice, the Mail app on old iOS often can't sign in to modern email accounts anymore. Gmail and many other providers no longer accept the simple password sign-in that these old Mail apps use, and some have dropped the old security protocols they rely on.

If an old account still works on your phone, this is fine for the odd photo. Expect it to fail with "Cannot Get Mail" or "Cannot Send Mail" otherwise. Mail also limits how many photos you can attach, and may shrink large videos.

RetroDrop's Mail route works around this. The phone "sends" to a mail server running on your own Mac, so no modern email account is involved.

4. Methods that no longer work Gone

  • My Photo Stream: Apple shut it down in 2023. Old phones that relied on it no longer sync anything.
  • iCloud Photos and AirDrop: neither ever supported iOS 6 or earlier.
  • iTunes / iPhoto sync: iTunes no longer exists on modern macOS, and syncing in Finder doesn't copy photos off the phone.
  • Photo transfer apps on the phone: apps like these did this well back in the day, but old iPhones can't download apps from the App Store anymore. Unless the app is already on your phone, this isn't an option.
  • Bluetooth: iPhones have never supported sending files over Bluetooth.
  • Uploading from Safari to websites: modern websites require security that old Safari doesn't support, and Safari couldn't upload files at all before iOS 6.

5. Jailbreaking For tinkerers

Jailbroken phones can share their files over SSH or file-sharing tweaks. It works, but jailbreaking an old phone takes time, carries a small risk of needing a restore, and isn't necessary just to get photos off. Back up your photos first if you go this route.
